## Report: Derivation of the Simplified 2D Magnetic Force Model

### 1. The General 3D Magnetic Dipole Model

The foundation for these models is the magnetic field generated by a point magnetic dipole. This is analogous to the electric field from an electric dipole.

#### 1.1. Magnetic Field (B)

The magnetic field $\mathbf{B}$ generated by a source magnet with a magnetic dipole moment $\mathbf{m}_s$ at a position vector $\mathbf{r}$ (from the source to the point of interest) is given by:

$$
\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{r}) = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi} \left( \frac{3\mathbf{r}(\mathbf{m}_s \cdot \mathbf{r})}{|\mathbf{r}|^5} - \frac{\mathbf{m}_s}{|\mathbf{r}|^3} \right)
$$
*(This formula is a standard result in magnetostatics and is referenced in sources like Abbott2020.pdf, page 4, eq. 9).*

Here:
*   $\mu_0$ is the magnetic permeability of free space ($4\pi \times 10^{-7}$ T·m/A).
*   $\mathbf{m}_s$ is the magnetic moment vector of the source magnet (a vector indicating its strength and orientation).
*   $\mathbf{r}$ is the position vector from the source magnet to the point where the field is being measured.
*   $|\mathbf{r}|$ is the magnitude (length) of the vector $\mathbf{r}$.

#### 1.2. Force (F) on another Dipole

In physics, systems tend to move to lower their potential energy. The force points in the direction of the steepest *decrease* in potential energy. Since potential energy is $U = -(\mathbf{b} \cdot \mathbf{m})$, the force $\mathbf{f} = -\nabla U = \nabla(\mathbf{b} \cdot \mathbf{m})$ points exactly where the object will be pushed.

When another magnet (e.g., a microrobot) with magnetic moment $\mathbf{m}_r$ is placed in this field, it experiences a force. The force $\mathbf{F}$ is the gradient of the potential energy ($U = \mathbf{m}_r \cdot \mathbf{B}$):

$$
\mathbf{F} = \nabla (\mathbf{m}_r \cdot \mathbf{B})
$$

This is a complete, but very complex, 3D formula. It's computationally expensive because it involves many vector operations (dot products, cross products) and the calculation of a gradient.

### 2. Simplification to the 2D Yousefi Model

The model in `Yousefi2021.pdf` is a powerful simplification based on a specific physical arrangement.

**Key Assumptions:**

1.  **Planar System:** All source magnets and the robot are constrained to the XY-plane. Therefore, the position vector $\mathbf{r}$ has no Z-component: $\mathbf{r} = [x, y, 0]$.
2.  **Vertical Magnetization:** The magnetic moments of *both* the source magnets and the robot are oriented purely along the Z-axis.
    *   Source moment: $\mathbf{m}_s = [0, 0, m_s]$
    *   Robot moment: $\mathbf{m}_r = [0, 0, m_r]$

Let's apply these assumptions step-by-step.

#### 2.1. Step 1: Simplify the Dot Product $(\mathbf{m}_s \cdot \mathbf{r})$

With $\mathbf{m}_s = [0, 0, m_s]$ and $\mathbf{r} = [x, y, 0]$:
$$
\mathbf{m}_s \cdot \mathbf{r} = (0 \cdot x) + (0 \cdot y) + (m_s \cdot 0) = 0
$$
This is a massive simplification!

#### 2.2. Step 2: Simplify the Magnetic Field Formula (B)

Substitute the result from Step 1 into the general formula for $\mathbf{B}$:

$$
\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{r}) = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi} \left( \frac{3\mathbf{r}(0)}{|\mathbf{r}|^5} - \frac{\mathbf{m}_s}{|\mathbf{r}|^3} \right) = - \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi} \frac{\mathbf{m}_s}{|\mathbf{r}|^3}
$$

Substituting the vectors:
$$
\mathbf{B}(x, y) = - \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi} \frac{[0, 0, m_s]}{(x^2 + y^2)^{3/2}}
$$
This shows that under these assumptions, the magnetic field at any point in the XY-plane points purely in the negative Z-direction.

#### 2.3. Step 3: Simplify the Potential Energy (U)

Now, we calculate the potential energy $U = \mathbf{m}_r \cdot \mathbf{B}$:

$$
U = [0, 0, m_r] \cdot \left( - \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi} \frac{[0, 0, m_s]}{(x^2 + y^2)^{3/2}} \right)
$$
The dot product multiplies the Z-components:
$$
U(x, y) = - \frac{\mu_0 m_s m_r}{4\pi} \frac{1}{(x^2 + y^2)^{3/2}} = - \frac{\mu_0 m_s m_r}{4\pi} |\mathbf{r}|^{-3}
$$

#### 2.4. Step 4: Derive the Force (F) from the Simplified Potential Energy

The force is the negative gradient of the potential energy, $\mathbf{F} = -\nabla U = -[\frac{\partial U}{\partial x}, \frac{\partial U}{\partial y}]$.

Let's define a constant $K = \frac{\mu_0 m_s m_r}{4\pi}$ for simplicity. So, $U = -K(x^2 + y^2)^{-3/2}$.

**Calculate the partial derivative with respect to x:**
$$
\frac{\partial U}{\partial x} = -K \left( -\frac{3}{2} (x^2 + y^2)^{-5/2} \cdot (2x) \right) = 3K \frac{x}{(x^2 + y^2)^{5/2}} = 3K \frac{x}{|\mathbf{r}|^5}
$$

**Calculate the partial derivative with respect to y:**
$$
\frac{\partial U}{\partial y} = -K \left( -\frac{3}{2} (x^2 + y^2)^{-5/2} \cdot (2y) \right) = 3K \frac{y}{(x^2 + y^2)^{5/2}} = 3K \frac{y}{|\mathbf{r}|^5}
$$

Now, assemble the force vector $\mathbf{F} = -[\frac{\partial U}{\partial x}, \frac{\partial U}{\partial y}]$:
$$
\mathbf{F} = - \left[ 3K \frac{x}{|\mathbf{r}|^5}, 3K \frac{y}{|\mathbf{r}|^5} \right] = -3K \frac{[x, y]}{|\mathbf{r}|^5} = -3K \frac{\mathbf{r}}{|\mathbf{r}|^5}
$$

This force is purely central (directed along $\mathbf{r}$) and is attractive (note the negative sign).

### 3. The Final Yousefi Model with Control Input

The derivation above assumes the source moment $m_s$ is constant. The Yousefi model introduces a control input $u_i$ for each source magnet `i`.

As stated in `Yousefi2021.pdf` (page 2, Section II-B), this input $u_i = \cos(\theta_i)$ represents the tilting of the magnet's moment. When $\theta_i=0$, the moment is fully vertical ($m_{s,z} = m_s$), and when $\theta_i=90^\circ$, the vertical component is zero. This input effectively scales the vertical component of the source magnet's moment.

To get the final formula, we make two changes:
1.  Replace the constant term $3K = \frac{3\mu_0 m_s m_r}{4\pi}$ with a single lumped constant $C_F$.
2.  Introduce the control input $u_i$ as a linear scaling factor.
3.  **Crucially, the Yousefi paper defines the force as repulsive.** This simply flips the sign from our derivation.

This leads to the final formula used in the paper (`Yousefi2021.pdf`, page 2, eq. 2):

$$
\mathbf{f}_m = C_F \cdot u_i \cdot \frac{\mathbf{r}}{|\mathbf{r}|^5}
$$

where:
*   $\mathbf{f}_m$ is the magnetic force in the XY-plane.
*   $C_F = \frac{3\mu_0 m_s m_r}{4\pi}$ is the force constant, combining all physical parameters.
*   $u_i$ is the dimensionless control input for the i-th magnet, where $u_i \in [-1, 1]$ allows for both attraction and repulsion, or $u_i \in [0, 1]$ for repulsion only.
*   $\mathbf{r}$ is the vector from the source magnet to the robot.

### Part 1: Refined Derivation Including the Control Angle $\theta$

The key is that the force on the robot `F` only depends on the **z-component** of the magnetic field, because the robot's own magnetic moment `m_r` is assumed to be purely vertical.

**Assumptions:**
1.  Robot's moment is vertical: $\mathbf{m}_r = [0, 0, m_r]$.
2.  The source magnet's moment `m_s` can be tilted by an angle $\theta$ with respect to the z-axis. This means the z-component of the source moment is $m_{sz} = |\mathbf{m}_s| \cos(\theta)$. The control input is defined as $u = \cos(\theta)$.
3.  The system is planar, so the position vector from source to robot is $\mathbf{r} = [x, y, 0]$.

#### Step 1: Force Depends Only on $B_z$

The force is the gradient of the interaction energy:
$$ \mathbf{F} = \nabla(\mathbf{m}_r \cdot \mathbf{B}) $$
Since $\mathbf{m}_r = [0, 0, m_r]$, the dot product picks out only the z-component of the field, $B_z$:
$$ \mathbf{m}_r \cdot \mathbf{B} = m_r B_z $$
Therefore, the force is simply the gradient of $B_z$, scaled by $m_r$:
$$ \mathbf{F} = \nabla(m_r B_z) = m_r \nabla B_z $$

#### Step 2: Find $B_z$ in the XY-Plane

Now we need to find $B_z$ using the general formula for the magnetic field:
$$ \mathbf{B}(\mathbf{r}) = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi} \left( \frac{3\mathbf{r}(\mathbf{m}_s \cdot \mathbf{r})}{|\mathbf{r}|^5} - \frac{\mathbf{m}_s}{|\mathbf{r}|^3} \right) $$
The z-component of this vector equation is:
$$ B_z = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi} \left( \frac{3 r_z (\mathbf{m}_s \cdot \mathbf{r})}{|\mathbf{r}|^5} - \frac{m_{sz}}{|\mathbf{r}|^3} \right) $$
Since we are in the XY-plane, the z-component of the position vector, $r_z$, is zero. This makes the entire first term disappear:
$$ B_z = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi} \left( 0 - \frac{m_{sz}}{|\mathbf{r}|^3} \right) = - \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi} \frac{m_{sz}}{|\mathbf{r}|^3} $$
This is a critical insight: **The vertical component of the magnetic field ($B_z$) in the XY-plane depends *only* on the vertical component of the source's moment ($m_{sz}$)**.

#### Step 3: Assemble the Force Formula

Now substitute $m_{sz} = |\mathbf{m}_s| \cos(\theta) = |\mathbf{m}_s| u$ into the equation for $B_z$:
$$ B_z = - \frac{\mu_0 |\mathbf{m}_s|}{4\pi} \frac{u}{|\mathbf{r}|^3} $$
The potential energy is $U = - (\mathbf{m}_r \cdot \mathbf{B}) = - m_r B_z$:
$$ U(x, y) = \frac{\mu_0 |\mathbf{m}_s| m_r}{4\pi} \frac{u}{|\mathbf{r}|^3} $$
The force is $\mathbf{F} = -\nabla U$. Taking the gradient of $U$ (as we did in the previous report) gives:
$$ \mathbf{F} = -\frac{3\mu_0 |\mathbf{m}_s| m_r}{4\pi} u \frac{\mathbf{r}}{|\mathbf{r}|^5} $$
This is an attractive force. The Yousefi model defines the force as repulsive and lumps the constants, giving us the final form:
$$ \mathbf{f}_m = C_F \cdot u \cdot \frac{\mathbf{r}}{|\mathbf{r}|^5} $$
This refined derivation shows that the control input $u$ is not just an ad-hoc scaling factor, but arises directly from the physics when a vertically-oriented robot magnet interacts with a tilting source magnet.

### Part 2: Hessian, Eigenvalues, and Stability

An equilibrium point is stable if it is at a local minimum of potential energy. This imposes conditions on the second derivatives of the energy, which are captured by the **Hessian matrix**.

#### Connection between Force and Potential Energy

*   Force: $\mathbf{F} = -\nabla U$
*   Jacobian of Force: $\mathbf{J}_F = \nabla \mathbf{F} = \nabla(-\nabla U) = -\nabla^2 U$
*   Hessian of Potential: $\mathbf{H}_U = \nabla^2 U$

Therefore, $\mathbf{J}_F = -\mathbf{H}_U$.

For a stable equilibrium (a minimum of $U$), the Hessian $\mathbf{H}_U$ must be **positive definite**. This is equivalent to the force Jacobian $\mathbf{J}_F$ being **negative definite**.

For a 2x2 matrix like our Jacobian, being negative definite requires:
1.  **Trace($\mathbf{J}_F$) < 0**
2.  **Determinant($\mathbf{J}_F$) > 0**

The eigenvalues ($\lambda_1, \lambda_2$) of the Jacobian must both be negative. The trace and determinant are related to the eigenvalues by:
*   Trace = $\lambda_1 + \lambda_2$
*   Determinant = $\lambda_1 \cdot \lambda_2$
If both eigenvalues are negative, their sum is negative (Trace < 0) and their product is positive (Determinant > 0).

#### Calculating the Jacobian of the Force Field

Let the repulsive force from a single source `i` be $\mathbf{f}_i = C_F u_i \frac{\mathbf{r}_i}{|\mathbf{r}_i|^5}$.
The Jacobian matrix for this single source, $\mathbf{J}_i = \nabla \mathbf{f}_i$, is:

$$ \mathbf{J}_i = \frac{C_F u_i}{|\mathbf{r}_i|^7} \begin{pmatrix} y_i^2 - 4x_i^2 & -5x_i y_i \\ -5x_i y_i & x_i^2 - 4y_i^2 \end{pmatrix} $$

The total Jacobian at an equilibrium point $\mathbf{p}_{des}$ is the sum of the Jacobians from all sources:
$$ \mathbf{J}_F = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \mathbf{J}_i(\mathbf{p}_{des}) $$

#### Analytical Look at a Single Source

We can gain physical insight by analyzing the trace and determinant for a *single* source's Jacobian:
*   **Trace($\mathbf{J}_i$)** = $\frac{C_F u_i}{|\mathbf{r}_i|^7} ((y_i^2 - 4x_i^2) + (x_i^2 - 4y_i^2)) = \frac{-3 C_F u_i}{|\mathbf{r}_i|^5}$
*   **Determinant($\mathbf{J}_i$)** = $\frac{-4 (C_F u_i)^2}{|\mathbf{r}_i|^{10}}$

Let's analyze this for a repulsive source ($u_i > 0$):
*   The trace is negative.
*   The determinant is **also negative**.

A negative determinant means the eigenvalues have opposite signs ($\lambda_1 > 0, \lambda_2 < 0$). This is the signature of a **saddle point**, not a stable minimum. This confirms a fundamental result from Earnshaw's theorem: **you cannot create a stable trap using a single static magnetic source.**

#### Stability with Multiple Sources

With multiple sources, the total Jacobian $\mathbf{J}_F$ is the sum of the individual matrices. The stability conditions must be applied to this final summed matrix.

1.  **Trace Condition:**
    $\text{Trace}(\mathbf{J}_F) = \sum_i \text{Trace}(\mathbf{J}_i) = \sum_i \frac{-3 C_F u_i}{|\mathbf{r}_i|^5} < 0$
    Since $-3C_F$ is a negative constant, this simplifies to:
    $$ \sum_i \frac{u_i}{|\mathbf{r}_i|^5} > 0 $$
    To create a potential well, you need a net "repulsive" character from the sources.

2.  **Determinant Condition:**
    $\text{Det}(\mathbf{J}_F) = \text{Det}(\sum_i \mathbf{J}_i) > 0$.
    Unlike the trace, the determinant of a sum is not the sum of the determinants. This expression is complex and must be computed numerically. The negative determinants of the individual sources must be overcome by the cross-terms that arise when summing the matrices. It is this interaction between the fields that makes a stable trap possible.

In summary, to check for stability in your optimization, you must:
1.  Construct the 2x2 Jacobian matrix for each source magnet.
2.  Sum them to get the total system Jacobian $\mathbf{J}_F$.
3.  Add two constraints to your optimizer:
    *   `Trace(J_F) < -epsilon` (where epsilon is a small positive number for numerical stability)
    *   `Determinant(J_F) > epsilon`








### 4. Multi-Robot Interactions and System Dynamics

To simulate a swarm of microrobots operating in close proximity, the physical model must be expanded to include both the mutual magnetic interactions between the agents and the fluid dynamics of their environment. 

#### 4.1. Inter-Robot Magnetic Interaction Force

When modeling multiple microrobots, they exert magnetic dipole-dipole forces on one another. We can apply the same assumptions used in the simplified 2D Yousefi model: all robots are in the XY-plane ($\mathbf{r}_{kj} = [x_{kj}, y_{kj}, 0]$) and have pure vertical magnetic moments ($\mathbf{m}_k = [0, 0, m_k]$ and $\mathbf{m}_j = [0, 0, m_j]$).

The magnetic field generated by robot $k$ at the position of robot $j$ (where $\mathbf{r}_{kj} = \mathbf{p}_j - \mathbf{p}_k$) is:
$$ \mathbf{B}_{kj} = -\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi} \frac{\mathbf{m}_k}{|\mathbf{r}_{kj}|^3} $$

The potential energy of robot $j$ resting in this field is $U_{kj} = - \mathbf{m}_j \cdot \mathbf{B}_{kj}$:
$$ U_{kj} = \frac{\mu_0 m_k m_j}{4\pi |\mathbf{r}_{kj}|^3} $$

The interaction force exerted on robot $j$ by robot $k$ is the negative gradient of this potential ($\mathbf{F}_{kj} = -\nabla U_{kj}$):
$$ \mathbf{F}_{kj} = \frac{3\mu_0 m_k m_j}{4\pi} \frac{\mathbf{r}_{kj}}{|\mathbf{r}_{kj}|^5} $$

Because the magnetic moments are parallel (both pointing in the $+Z$ direction), this simplified inter-agent force is purely **repulsive**. It pushes the robots apart along the vector $\mathbf{r}_{kj}$, which is why the interaction forces prevent the microrobots from easily colliding and clumping together.


The interaction force between two magnetic dipoles $m_1$ and $m_2$ separated by a distance vector $r$ is given by the standard dipole-dipole interaction equation:
$$ F = \frac{3\mu_0}{4\pi |r|^5} \left[ (m_1 \cdot r)m_2 + (m_2 \cdot r)m_1 + (m_1 \cdot m_2)r - \frac{5(m_1 \cdot r)(m_2 \cdot r)}{|r|^2} r \right] $$


#### 4.2. Equations of Motion (ODE Formulation)

Microrobots typically operate in low-Reynolds-number fluid environments (like water or blood), meaning viscous fluid drag is highly dominant. The total net force on the $j$-th robot is the sum of the external control forces from the permanent magnets, the interaction forces from all other $k$ robots, and the opposing fluid drag.

The governing differential equation (Newton's Second Law) for the $j$-th robot is:
$$ m_j \ddot{\mathbf{p}}_j = \mathbf{F}_{ext, j} + \sum_{k \neq j} \mathbf{F}_{kj} - c \dot{\mathbf{p}}_j $$

Where:
*   $\mathbf{p}_j = [x_j, y_j]$ is the position vector of the $j$-th robot.
*   $m_j$ is the mass of the $j$-th robot.
*   $c$ is the viscous drag coefficient of the fluid.
*   $\mathbf{F}_{ext, j} = \sum_{i} C_{F,i} \cdot u_i \cdot \frac{\mathbf{r}_{ij}}{|\mathbf{r}_{ij}|^5}$ is the total external control force on robot $j$ from all source magnets $i$.
*   $\mathbf{F}_{kj}$ is the repulsive interaction force from robot $k$ on robot $j$.

To simulate this dynamic system numerically (e.g., using a Runge-Kutta solver), the second-order differential equation must be transformed into a first-order Ordinary Differential Equation (ODE) state-space model. For $N$ robots, the state vector contains $4N$ elements representing positions and velocities:
$$ \frac{d}{dt} \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{p}_j \\ \dot{\mathbf{p}}_j \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \dot{\mathbf{p}}_j \\ \frac{1}{m_j} \left( \mathbf{F}_{ext, j} + \sum_{k \neq j} \mathbf{F}_{kj} - c \dot{\mathbf{p}}_j \right) \end{bmatrix} $$
