Yes, absolutely. You've pointed out some very important and subtle details in vector calculus notation. Let's break it down.

All three expressions for the force are equivalent under the standard conditions of this problem.

1.  `f = ∇(b . m)`
2.  `f = (m . ∇)b`
3.  My formula: `F = ∇(m_r . B)`

Here is why they are all the same.

### 1. Commutativity of the Dot Product

First, you asked if `∇(b . m)` is the same as `∇(m . b)`.

*   **Yes, they are identical.**
*   The dot product of two vectors is a scalar, and it is commutative. This means that for any two vectors $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$, the scalar result of $\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b}$ is exactly the same as $\mathbf{b} \cdot \mathbf{a}$.
*   Therefore, the scalar potential energy field $U = -(\mathbf{b} \cdot \mathbf{m})$ is identical to $U = -(\mathbf{m} \cdot \mathbf{b})$. Taking the gradient of either expression gives the same force vector.

### 2. The Vector Calculus Identity: `∇(m . b)` vs. `(m . ∇)b`

This is the most interesting part of your question. The image correctly states that these two forms are equal.
$$
\mathbf{f} = \nabla(\mathbf{m} \cdot \mathbf{b}) = (\mathbf{m} \cdot \nabla)\mathbf{b}
$$
This is a standard vector calculus identity that holds true under two key assumptions that apply here:
1.  The magnetic moment $\mathbf{m}$ of the object experiencing the force is treated as a **constant vector**. It does not change with position.
2.  The magnetic field $\mathbf{b}$ is generated by a permanent magnet (or a DC current) in a region with no other currents. In physics, this means the field is **curl-free**: $\nabla \times \mathbf{b} = \mathbf{0}$.

When these conditions are met, the two notations describe the exact same mathematical operation, as shown in the expanded forms in equations (12) and (13) in your image. The reason they look different in their component form is that they group the derivatives differently, but because the field's Jacobian matrix is symmetric (a direct result of being curl-free), the final result is identical.

### 3. Your Formula vs. The Image's Formula

Finally, you asked if the formula from the image is the same as the one I've used:
$$
\mathbf{F} = \nabla (\mathbf{m}_r \cdot \mathbf{B})
$$

*   **Yes, this is exactly the same formula.**
*   The only difference is the choice of variable names, which is just a matter of convention.

| My Notation | Image Notation | Meaning |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\mathbf{F}$ | $\mathbf{f}$ | Force vector |
| $\nabla$ | $\nabla$ | Gradient operator |
| $\mathbf{m}_r$ | $\mathbf{m}$ | Magnetic moment of the robot/object |
| $\mathbf{B}$ | $\mathbf{b}$ | Magnetic field vector from the source |

So, `f = ∇(b . m)` is the direct equivalent of `F = ∇(B . m_r)`. All the physics and mathematics are identical.
